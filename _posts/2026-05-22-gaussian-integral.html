--- title: "The Gaussian Integral: Squaring the Circle of Infinite Area" date: 2026-05-22 permalink: /posts/2026/05/gaussian-integral-polar/ tags: - mathematics - calculus - integration - analysis author_profile: true --- At its heart, the Gaussian integral asks a single question: **what is the area under the bell curve $e^{-x^2}$ from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$?** The function $f(x) = e^{-x^2}$ has no elementary antiderivative — you cannot write a closed-form expression for $\int e^{-x^2}\,dx$ in terms of familiar functions. Yet the *definite* integral over all of $\mathbb{R}$ has the stunningly clean value $\sqrt{\pi}$. This apparent miracle is why mathematicians have been enchanted by it for nearly three centuries. > **🗓️ Historical note** > The result is attributed to **Carl Friedrich Gauss** (1777–1855), who used it in his work on the normal distribution. The method we present — squaring the integral and switching to polar coordinates — appears to have been first published by **Poisson** in 1823. The function is symmetric ($e^{-x^2} = e^{-(-x)^2}$), always positive, and decays rapidly to zero as $|x| \to \infty$. These properties guarantee the integral converges. --- ## The Polar Coordinates Proof The standard proof is one of the most elegant tricks in analysis. We cannot integrate the Gaussian directly — so instead, we *square* it and convert to polar coordinates. ### Step 1: Define $I$ Let $I$ be the integral we want to evaluate: $$\text{Definition: } I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx$$ ### Step 2: Compute $I^2$ Write $I^2$ as a product of two independent integrals (using a dummy variable $y$): $$\text{Key Insight: } I^2 = \left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx\right)\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y^2}\,dy\right) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy$$ This is now a **double integral over the entire plane**. ### Step 3: Switch to Polar Coordinates The integrand $e^{-(x^2+y^2)}$ depends only on $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$ — it has perfect circular symmetry. This calls for **polar coordinates**: $$\text{Substitution: } x = r\cos\theta,\quad y = r\sin\theta,\quad dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$$ The Cartesian plane maps to $r \in [0, \infty)$ and $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$. ### Step 4: Evaluate the Double Integral $$\text{Evaluation: } I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi \int_0^{\infty} r\,e^{-r^2}\,dr$$ Now substitute $u = r^2$, so $du = 2r\,dr$: $$\text{u-substitution: } I^2 = 2\pi \cdot \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{\infty} e^{-u}\,du = \pi \Big[-e^{-u}\Big]_0^{\infty} = \pi(0 - (-1)) = \pi$$ ### Step 5: Take the Square Root Since $I > 0$ (the integrand is positive), we take the positive square root: $$\text{The Gaussian Integral: } I^2 = \pi \implies I = \sqrt{\pi}$$ > **✦ Why does the trick work?** > The magic lies entirely within the Jacobian factor $r$ generated by switching to polar coordinates. By squaring $I$, we explicitly introduce the variable $r$ alongside $e^{-r^2}$, turning an impossible antiderivative problem into a basic Calculus 1 $u$-substitution problem. --- ## Visualizing the Integral The Gaussian curve is the iconic bell shape. Its area under the curve equals $\sqrt{\pi} \approx 1.7725$. ### The 3D Perspective To understand the polar-coordinates proof geometrically: the product $I^2$ represents the **volume under the surface $z = e^{-(x^2+y^2)}$** over the entire plane. This bell-shaped mound has volume $\pi$, so each "slice" through its cross-section gives area $\sqrt{\pi}$. The radial integrand can be visualized as $r\,e^{-r^2}$. The area under this curve equals $\frac{1}{2}$, which satisfies the relation $I^2 = 2\pi \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \pi$. --- ## Solving with Python Python offers several distinct approaches: symbolic computation, numerical quadrature, Monte Carlo estimation, and direct use of the special-functions library.
