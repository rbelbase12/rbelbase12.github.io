The Gibbs Phenomenon of the Square Wave

Why adding more and more sine waves never removes the overshoot at a jump, and exactly how big that overshoot is.

1. Periodic functions and the square wave

A function $f$ is periodic with period $2\pi$ if $f(x+2\pi)=f(x)$ for all $x$. Sines and cosines such as $\sin(kx)$ are the basic examples. Our test function is the square wave:

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}-1,& -\piIt is smooth everywhere except at the multiples of $\pi$, where it jumps by $2$. That jump is where all the interesting behaviour happens.

2. Fourier series

The idea of Fourier analysis is to write a periodic function as a sum of sines and cosines. For a $2\pi$-periodic $f$,

$$f(x)\sim \frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big(a_k\cos kx+b_k\sin kx\big),$$ $$a_k=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\cos kx\,dx,\qquad b_k=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\sin kx\,dx.$$

The square wave is odd, so every $a_k=0$. For the sine coefficients,

$$b_k=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\sin kx\,dx=\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{1-\cos k\pi}{k}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{4}{\pi k},& k\text{ odd},\\[2mm] 0,& k\text{ even}.\end{cases}$$
$$f(x)\sim\frac{4}{\pi}\left(\sin x+\frac{\sin 3x}{3}+\frac{\sin 5x}{5}+\cdots\right)=\frac4\pi\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin\big((2j-1)x\big)}{2j-1}.$$

The partial sum with $m$ nonzero terms is $S_m(x)=\frac4\pi\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\sin((2j-1)x)}{2j-1}$. Away from the jumps, $S_m(x)\to f(x)$. What happens near a jump?

5. Where the number comes from

Differentiate $S_m$ and use $\sum_{j=1}^m\cos((2j-1)x)=\dfrac{\sin 2mx}{2\sin x}$:

$$S_m'(x)=\frac4\pi\sum_{j=1}^{m}\cos\big((2j-1)x\big)=\frac{2}{\pi}\,\frac{\sin 2mx}{\sin x}.$$

This vanishes first at $x=\frac{\pi}{2m}$, which is the peak. Integrating from $0$ gives its height:

$$S_m\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2m}\right)=\frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2m}\frac{\sin 2mt}{\sin t}\,dt\;\xrightarrow{\,m\to\infty\,}\;\frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi}\frac{\sin u}{u}\,du=\frac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{Si}(\pi)\approx 1.17898.$$

Here we substituted $u=2mt$ and used $\sin t\approx t$ for small $t$. The true value at the top of the jump is $1$, so the overshoot is $0.17898$. Relative to the jump size $2$, that is

$$\frac{\tfrac2\pi\operatorname{Si}(\pi)-1}{2}\approx 0.0895,$$

about 9% of the jump, for every large $m$. This is the Gibbs phenomenon. The convergence $S_m\to f$ is pointwise away from the jump but not uniform near it.

6. Can we get rid of it?

Switch the method in section 3. Both fixes multiply the $k$-th term by a damping factor before summing, with $M$ the highest harmonic used:

$$\text{Lanczos: } \sigma_k=\frac{\sin\big(\pi k/(M+1)\big)}{\pi k/(M+1)},\qquad \text{Fejér: } \sigma_k=1-\frac{k}{M+1}.$$

Both remove the overshoot, at the price of a blurrier jump. This trade-off between sharp edges and ringing is the starting point for more refined tools, such as concentration factors for edge detection and prolate spheroidal wave functions.
